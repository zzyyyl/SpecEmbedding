\begin{abstract}
Tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) is a key technology in metabolomics, yet
annotating unknown spectra remains difficult because many structurally distinct
molecules share similar precursor masses, molecular formulas, and fragmentation
patterns. We study MS/MS-based metabolite annotation as a data mining problem
for bioinformatics: given an observed spectrum and a candidate molecular
library, the goal is to mine a large chemical space and rank the true molecule
as high as possible. We propose \method{}, a two-stage candidate-constrained
framework that first learns aligned spectrum and molecule embeddings for coarse
retrieval and then applies a lightweight listwise molecular reranker to refine
hard candidates. The reranker combines spectrum embeddings, molecule embeddings,
base retrieval scores, rank embeddings, and candidate-candidate context, thereby
improving fine-grained ranking without generating new molecular structures.
Preliminary MassSpecGym experiments show that reranking substantially improves
Top-1 accuracy and mean reciprocal rank over the base retrieval model under both
mass-constrained and formula-constrained candidate libraries. The study further
emphasizes fair candidate construction, candidate recall upper bounds, and
ablation protocols, aligning the work with advanced data mining, learning to
rank, and bioinformatics applications.
\keywords{Data mining for bioinformatics \and Tandem mass spectrometry \and
Metabolite annotation \and Molecular retrieval \and Learning to rank \and
Representation learning}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Untargeted metabolomics aims to discover and interpret small molecules from
complex biological samples. MS/MS spectra provide informative fragmentation
patterns, but reliable molecular annotation is still challenging. Different
molecules may have similar masses or formulas, and the same molecule can produce
different spectra under different instruments, collision energies, and adducts.
Consequently, MS/MS annotation is not only a chemistry problem but also an
advanced data mining task: a method must search, filter, and rank candidates in
a large, noisy, and partially observed chemical space.

Recent neural approaches learn spectrum embeddings, molecular embeddings, or
joint spectrum-molecule embedding spaces. These models are efficient for
large-scale retrieval, but their top-ranked results often contain hard negative
molecules that are chemically close to the correct molecule. This observation is
important for ADMA's data mining scope: the task is naturally a learning-to-rank
problem over structured scientific data, where the quality of candidate mining
and the quality of candidate ordering must be evaluated separately.

We propose \method{}, a candidate-constrained molecular reranking framework for
MS/MS-based metabolite annotation. The framework first uses a spectrum-molecule
alignment model to retrieve top-$K$ candidate molecules from a mass- or
formula-constrained library. It then applies a listwise reranker that re-scores
the candidates using pairwise spectrum-molecule features, base retrieval scores,
base ranks, and candidate-candidate context. Unlike generative retrieval methods,
our reranker does not produce new molecular structures; it directly optimizes the
ordering of already retrieved candidates. This makes the method lightweight,
transparent, and easy to deploy as a second-stage ranking module.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{MS/MS-Based Molecular Annotation.}
Traditional annotation methods rely on spectral library search, fragmentation
rules, molecular fingerprint prediction, or formula-guided candidate filtering.
They are effective when reference spectra are available, but they are limited by
library coverage and often struggle with structurally similar candidates. Recent
benchmarks such as MassSpecGym encourage more systematic evaluation of
spectrum-to-molecule retrieval under controlled candidate libraries.

\paragraph{Representation Learning for Spectra and Molecules.}
Deep learning methods encode spectra with peak-sequence models, Transformer
encoders, or binned representations, and encode molecules with molecular graphs,
SMILES encoders, or chemical fingerprints. Contrastive learning can align the two
modalities in a shared embedding space for efficient retrieval, but modality
misalignment remains a major source of top-ranked errors.

\paragraph{Learning to Rank for Scientific Data Mining.}
Learning-to-rank methods are widely used in search and recommendation. In MS/MS
annotation, candidates for the same spectrum are not independent: they share mass
constraints, formula constraints, and structural motifs. This motivates a
listwise reranker that uses the full candidate set as context rather than scoring
each candidate independently.

\paragraph{Generative Retrieval and Non-generative Reranking.}
Recent generative retrieval frameworks use a molecule generator to bridge the
spectrum-molecule modality gap and then rerank candidates by molecular
similarity. Our work studies a complementary question: how far can a simpler
non-generative reranker go if the initial candidate set already contains strong
hard candidates? This comparison is important because generation can improve
accuracy but also increases inference cost and introduces validity constraints.

\section{Problem Formulation}

Let $s$ denote an MS/MS spectrum and $m$ denote a candidate molecule. Given a
candidate molecular library $\mathcal{D}$, the retrieval goal is to rank the true
molecule $m^+$ as high as possible:

\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{rank}(m^{+} \mid s, \mathcal{D}) \rightarrow 1.
\end{equation}

In practical metabolite annotation, the searchable library is usually filtered
by experimental metadata, such as precursor mass, adduct type, or molecular
formula. We therefore define a per-spectrum candidate set
$\candidates_s \subseteq \mathcal{D}$. The reranking problem is to improve the
order of candidates inside $\candidates_s$. If $m^+ \notin \candidates_s$, no
reranker can recover the correct molecule. We report the candidate recall upper
bound to separate candidate-generation failure from ranking failure.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Overview}

\method{} follows a two-stage pipeline. The first stage performs coarse
spectrum-molecule retrieval using aligned embeddings. The second stage performs
candidate-aware listwise reranking over the retrieved top-$K$ molecules.

\subsection{Spectrum-Molecule Alignment Model}

The base model maps spectrum $s$ to an embedding $\zs$ and molecule $m$ to an
embedding $\zm$. We compute the base retrieval score by normalized embedding
similarity:

\begin{equation}
  b(s, m) = \zs^{\top}\zm.
\end{equation}

For each spectrum, the top-$K$ candidates with the highest base scores are kept
for reranking. The base stage provides candidate molecules, base scores, and
base ranks.

\subsection{Candidate Reranker}

For each candidate molecule $m_i$, we construct the pairwise feature vector:

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{x}_i =
  [\zs,\ \mathbf{z}_{m_i},\ \zs \odot \mathbf{z}_{m_i},\
  |\zs - \mathbf{z}_{m_i}|,\ b_i,\ \mathbf{e}(r_i)],
\end{equation}

where $b_i$ is the base score, $r_i$ is the base rank, and $\mathbf{e}(r_i)$ is a
learnable rank embedding. The feature vector is projected by a multilayer
perceptron:

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{h}_i = \operatorname{MLP}(\mathbf{x}_i).
\end{equation}

The sequence $[\mathbf{h}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{h}_K]$ is then passed to a
Transformer encoder to model candidate-candidate context:

\begin{equation}
  [\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_1,\ldots,\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_K]
  = \operatorname{Transformer}([\mathbf{h}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{h}_K]).
\end{equation}

The reranker predicts a residual score $\Delta_i$ and combines it with the base
retrieval score:

\begin{equation}
  s_i = \alpha b_i + \Delta_i,
\end{equation}

where $\alpha$ is a learnable scalar. Candidates are sorted by $s_i$.

\subsection{Training Objective}

Let $y$ be the index of the true molecule in the candidate list. We optimize a
listwise cross-entropy loss:

\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}
  = -\log \frac{\exp(s_y)}{\sum_{i=1}^{K}\exp(s_i)}.
\end{equation}

We also use a pairwise ranking loss to separate the positive molecule from hard
negative candidates:

\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{pair}}
  = \frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{i \neq y}
  \log \left(1 + \exp(s_i - s_y + \gamma)\right),
\end{equation}

where $\gamma$ is a margin. The final loss is:

\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}} +
  \lambda \mathcal{L}_{\text{pair}}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Candidate Construction Protocol}

To comply with a fair data mining evaluation, the main experiments will use
candidate sets that are available without using test labels, such as official
MassSpecGym retrieval libraries or candidates generated from experimental mass
metadata. Any oracle setting that inserts the ground-truth SMILES into the test
candidate pool will be clearly separated from the main results. For each run, we
will report the candidate source, $K$, candidate pool size, and labeled-query
fraction.

\subsection{Baselines}

We plan to compare against the following baselines:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Traditional spectral similarity or library search methods.
  \item Neural spectrum-to-molecule retrieval models such as MIST, CMSSP, and
  JESTR, when reproducible results are available.
  \item Recent generative retrieval methods such as GLMR, using reported or
  reproduced MassSpecGym settings.
  \item Our base spectrum-molecule retrieval model without reranking.
  \item Pointwise reranking without candidate-candidate context.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

We report Recall@1/5/10/20 or Top-$K$ accuracy, mean reciprocal rank (MRR), and
candidate recall upper bound. We also plan to report MCES@1 to evaluate the
structural similarity between the top-ranked prediction and the ground truth. To
highlight the practical data mining trade-off, we will include inference time and
memory cost for the base model, reranker, and generative baselines.

\section{Ablation Studies}

\subsection{Effect of Reranking}

We compare the base retrieval model with \method{} to measure the gain from the
second-stage learning-to-rank module.

\subsection{Reranker Architecture}

We compare the Transformer reranker with a pointwise reranker and vary the
number of Transformer layers, attention heads, hidden dimensions, and rank
embedding dimensions.

\subsection{Feature Ablation}

We remove base score, base rank embedding, element-wise product, and absolute
difference features to determine which signals contribute most to hard candidate
discrimination.

\subsection{Loss Function Ablation}

We compare listwise cross-entropy alone with the combined cross-entropy and
pairwise ranking objective.

\subsection{Candidate Set Analysis}

We evaluate mass candidates, formula candidates, official benchmark candidates,
external database candidates, and different values of $K$. This analysis is
important because reranking improvements must be interpreted relative to the
candidate recall upper bound.

\section{Discussion}

\method{} is designed for candidate-constrained bioinformatics data mining. Its
main advantage is that it improves hard candidate ordering without molecular
generation, making it cheaper and easier to integrate into existing retrieval
pipelines. Its main limitation is that it cannot recover the correct molecule if
the initial retrieval stage fails to include it. Therefore, the final ADMA
submission will report candidate upper bounds, non-oracle candidate protocols,
MCES@1, and inference cost to avoid overstating the scope of the reranker.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented \method{}, a two-stage framework for MS/MS-based metabolite
annotation. By combining spectrum-molecule embedding retrieval with listwise
molecular reranking, the method addresses a practical data mining problem in
bioinformatics: ranking hard molecular candidates under realistic mass or formula
constraints. Future work will focus on strict non-oracle candidate construction,
cross-dataset generalization, and large-library deployment.
